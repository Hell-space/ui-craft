The Machines That Knew the Sea

For a century, the tides of the world’s harbours were foretold by brass computers the size of wardrobes. A short history of predicting the ocean by turning a crank.

By Maren Okafor with figures by the author 10 Nov 2026 9 min read

There is a moment, twice a day in most harbours, when the sea seems to forget what it was doing. The ebb has run out; the flood has not yet begun. Sailors call it slack water, and for most of human history the only way to know when it would come was to have watched it come before — to keep a book, year after year, and trust that the moon would keep its appointments.

The moon does keep them, more or less. But “more or less” sinks ships. A tide that turns forty minutes later than the almanac promised can leave a laden collier sitting on the bar at the mouth of a river, or float a stack of timber off a beach that was meant to be dry. By the middle of the nineteenth century, with steam shipping multiplying and harbour schedules tightening, the question of exactly when, and how high, the water would stand had become a commercial problem — and, for a few people, a beautiful mathematical one.

I A sea made of clocks

The beautiful version goes like this. The tide at any port is not one wave but many, stacked. The moon pulls the ocean into a bulge that sweeps round the earth roughly every twelve hours and twenty-five minutes; the sun does the same, more weakly, every twelve hours exactly. Because the two periods differ, they drift in and out of step: when they agree you get the big spring tides, when they quarrel you get the sluggish neaps. Add the tilt of the moon’s orbit, its elliptical path, the sun’s own drift through the seasons, and you arrive at dozens of distinct rhythms, each a clean sine wave with its own period.

Those periods are set by astronomy and are the same everywhere. What differs from port to port is how much of each wave arrives, and how late. The shape of the coastline, the depth of the shelf, the funnel of an estuary — all of it rescales and delays each component. Measure a year of real water levels at a harbour, pull the record apart into its constituents,The process is called harmonic analysis. Kelvin designed a separate machine to do it mechanically, but for decades most of it was done by hand, on printed forms, over very long afternoons. and you are left with a short list of numbers: for each rhythm, an amplitude and a phase. Those numbers are the port’s signature. With them you can, in principle, run the sea forward as far as you like.

In principle. In practice, adding together twenty or thirty sine waves at every hour of every day of a year is a staggering amount of arithmetic. Human computers did it, slowly, and made mistakes. What was wanted was a device that could do the adding by itself.

II Turning the crank

It came from William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, who in 1872 worked out a way to sum the waves mechanically. The trick was disarmingly simple. Each constituent tide is represented by a pulley mounted on a crank. Turn the crank and the pulley rises and falls in a perfect sine. Gear the cranks so that each turns at its constituent’s proper speed, then thread a single fine wire under and over all the pulleys in turn, with one end fixed and the other holding a pen. Every pulley tugs on the wire by its own amount. The pen, at the free end, feels the total.

The first machine handled ten constituents and drew its curve on a roll of paper while an operator wound a handle. A year of tides for one port could be traced in an afternoon. That number is the whole revolution. Tide tables stopped being a record of what the sea had done and became a forecast of what it would do, produced on demand.

Other nations built their own. In the United States, William Ferrel’s machine of 1882 went further, indicating the times and heights of high and low water directly instead of leaving them to be read off a curve. Its successor, designed at the Coast and Geodetic Survey and finished around 1912, summed thirty-seven constituents and weighed well over a ton. The staff called it Old Brass Brains.It survives, and is held by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Setting it up for a new port meant adjusting every crank by hand, which took longer than the run itself. It stayed in service until the mid-1960s, when electronic computers finally took over a job it had done, without complaint, for half a century.

Sidebar

How to read a tide table

A tide table gives the times and heights of each high and low water. Heights are measured not from mean sea level but from chart datum, a deliberately low reference, so that a depth printed on a chart is the least you should expect to find.

Two cautions. Times are usually printed in standard time, so add an hour in summer. And between the printed extremes, the water does not move evenly: by the old rule of twelfths, it rises one twelfth of its range in the first hour, two in the second, three in each of the third and fourth, then two, then one.

III The Normandy question

The most consequential tide prediction ever made came off machines like these. In 1943 the Allied planners preparing to land in France needed to know, for a coast they could not survey openly, when the water would be low enough to expose the beach obstacles but rising fast enough to float landing craft in behind them. The work went, discreetly, to Arthur Doodson at the Liverpool Tidal Institute, who was handed a set of harmonic constants without being told where they came from.Doodson is said to have worked out, from the constants alone, roughly which stretch of coast he was predicting. He kept it to himself. He ran them through two machines and sent back the tables. The window they described is one reason the landings fell in the first week of June — and one reason they so nearly did not, when the weather broke.

There is something moving in that picture: a man in a provincial institute, turning brass gears in wartime, rehearsing a sea he had never seen for a reason he was not allowed to know. The ocean, it turned out, could be rehearsed in a workshop. That was the deep claim of Kelvin’s machine, more than any of its gearing: that something as large and wild as a tide was, underneath, a sum of clocks.

IV What the machine could not see

It was never the whole truth, and the people who used these machines knew it. A tide predictor computes the astronomical tide, the part driven by the moon and sun. It knows nothing of weather. A strong onshore gale or a deep area of low pressure can pile water against a coast a metre or more above the prediction. The North Sea flood of 1953, which killed more than two thousand people in the Netherlands and Britain, was a storm surge riding on a spring tide that every table had correctly foreseen. The machines were right, and it did not help.

“The ocean, it turned out, could be rehearsed in a workshop.”

Modern forecasting stitches the two halves together: harmonic predictions for the astronomical part, numerical weather models for the surge. The harmonics are now computed in milliseconds, and the constants for a major port can run past a hundred terms. But the method is still Kelvin’s. Open the tide app on your phone and you are looking at the output of a calculation he would recognise at once — a few dozen clocks, each with its amplitude and phase, added up hour by hour.

Several of the old machines survive, in museums in Liverpool, London, Washington and elsewhere, mostly under glass and mostly not running. It is worth finding one. They are beautiful in the plain way that honest instruments often are, all exposed gears and taut wire, and they make visible something a screen hides: that prediction, at least of this kind, is neither mystery nor guess. It is a mechanism. You could stand beside it with your hand on the crank and watch the future come out of the end.
